Impact of Artificial Intelligence on Modern Software Engineering Practices: A Comprehensive Survey

Nawal Fateh

Department of Computer Science

Air University

Islamabad, Pakistan

nawalfateh@mail.au.edu.pk

Abstract—

The rapid advancement of Artificial Intelligence (AI) has significantly transformed the landscape of software engineering. This paper presents a comprehensive survey of AI-driven techniques and their applications across various phases of the software development lifecycle (SDLC), including requirements analysis, code generation, testing, debugging, and deployment. We examine the integration of machine learning models, natural language processing, and large language models (LLMs) into modern development workflows, evaluating their effectiveness in improving developer productivity, code quality, and software reliability. Our findings indicate that AI-assisted tools can reduce development time by up to 40% while maintaining comparable code quality metrics. However, challenges persist in areas such as model interpretability, bias mitigation, and the need for domain-specific training data. This survey provides actionable insights for practitioners and researchers seeking to leverage AI in software engineering.

Keywords—artificial intelligence, software engineering, machine learning, code generation, large language models, software testing, developer productivity

I. Introduction

Software engineering has undergone a paradigm shift with the emergence of Artificial Intelligence technologies. Traditional development methodologies, while still foundational, are increasingly being augmented and, in some cases, replaced by AI-driven approaches that promise greater efficiency, accuracy, and scalability [1]. The integration of AI into software engineering is not merely an incremental improvement but represents a fundamental transformation in how software is conceived, developed, tested, and maintained.

The proliferation of Large Language Models (LLMs) such as GPT-4, Codex, and their successors has introduced capabilities that were previously considered beyond the reach of automated systems. These models demonstrate proficiency in code generation, bug detection, documentation synthesis, and even architectural design recommendations [2]. According to recent industry reports, over 70% of professional developers now use AI-assisted coding tools in their daily workflows, marking a significant shift from traditional development practices.

This paper aims to provide a structured analysis of current AI applications in software engineering, identify key challenges and limitations, and propose directions for future research. Our survey encompasses peer-reviewed literature from 2020 to 2026, industry case studies, and empirical evaluations of commercially available AI-powered development tools.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Section II reviews related work in the field. Section III describes our methodology for conducting this survey. Section IV presents our findings across various dimensions. Section V discusses implications and limitations, and Section VI concludes with future directions.

II. Related Work

The intersection of AI and software engineering has been explored extensively in recent years. Feldt et al. [3] provided an early framework for understanding the potential of AI in software testing, establishing taxonomies that remain relevant today. Their work identified automated test generation and fault localization as primary areas where machine learning could yield significant improvements.

Chen et al. [4] introduced Codex, a GPT-based model fine-tuned for code generation, demonstrating that large-scale language models could achieve functional correctness on programming benchmarks at rates approaching human-level performance. This work catalyzed a wave of research into LLM-based code assistants, culminating in tools such as GitHub Copilot and Amazon CodeWhisperer.

Recent work by Zhang and colleagues [5] has focused on the reliability of AI-generated code, revealing that while LLMs produce syntactically correct code in over 90% of cases, semantic correctness—particularly for complex algorithms and edge cases—remains a significant challenge. Their findings underscore the necessity of human-in-the-loop validation in AI-assisted development workflows.

In the domain of requirements engineering, Dalpiaz and Brinkkemper [6] demonstrated that NLP techniques could effectively extract and classify software requirements from natural language documents, achieving F1 scores above 0.85 for requirement categorization tasks.

III. Methodology

We conducted a systematic literature review (SLR) following the guidelines proposed by Kitchenham and Charters [7]. Our search strategy encompassed major digital libraries including IEEE Xplore, ACM Digital Library, Springer, and arXiv. We employed the following search string:

("artificial intelligence" OR "machine learning" OR "deep learning")
AND ("software engineering" OR "software development")
AND ("code generation" OR "testing" OR "debugging" OR "deployment")

A. Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Studies were included if they: (1) presented empirical evidence of AI application in software engineering, (2) were published between 2020 and 2026, and (3) were peer-reviewed or published in reputable preprint archives. Studies focusing exclusively on theoretical frameworks without empirical validation were excluded.

B. Data Extraction

From each selected study, we extracted: the AI technique employed, the software engineering phase targeted, reported performance metrics, dataset characteristics, and identified limitations. A total of 147 primary studies met our inclusion criteria after initial screening and quality assessment.

IV. Results and Discussion

A. Code Generation

AI-powered code generation has emerged as the most actively researched area, accounting for 28.6% of the surveyed studies. Modern LLMs demonstrate remarkable capability in generating boilerplate code, implementing standard algorithms, and translating between programming languages. Our analysis reveals that AI-assisted code generation tools improve developer productivity by 25-55% for routine coding tasks, as measured by lines of code per hour and task completion time [8].

However, the quality of generated code varies significantly across complexity levels. For simple function implementations, AI models achieve functional correctness rates exceeding 85%. This rate decreases to approximately 45% for complex, multi-module implementations requiring deep understanding of system architecture [9].

B. Software Testing

AI-driven testing approaches have shown substantial promise in test case generation, mutation testing, and regression test optimization. Studies report that ML-based test generation tools achieve code coverage improvements of 15-30% compared to traditional random testing approaches [10]. Additionally, reinforcement learning techniques have been successfully applied to GUI testing, with agents learning to explore application states more efficiently than script-based approaches.

Fuzzing techniques enhanced by neural networks have demonstrated superior vulnerability detection capabilities, identifying 22% more unique bugs compared to coverage-guided fuzzing alone in controlled experiments [11].

C. Bug Detection and Debugging

Static analysis augmented by deep learning models has improved false positive rates in bug detection by approximately 35% compared to traditional rule-based analyzers [12]. Graph neural networks (GNNs) applied to program dependency graphs have shown particular effectiveness in detecting complex bug patterns that span multiple code regions. Recent work demonstrates that transformer-based models can predict bug locations with precision scores above 0.75 for common bug categories.

D. Developer Productivity

A meta-analysis of 23 empirical studies measuring developer productivity reveals consistent improvements when AI tools are integrated into development workflows. The median reported improvement is 32% in task completion time, with the highest gains observed in code review (45% faster) and documentation generation (60% faster). Notably, productivity gains are most pronounced for junior developers, suggesting that AI tools serve an important role in reducing the expertise gap [13].

V. Challenges and Limitations

Despite the promising results, several significant challenges remain. First, the interpretability of AI-generated code and decisions presents ongoing concerns for safety-critical systems [14]. Developers report difficulty in understanding why a model suggests specific code patterns, which hampers trust and adoption in high-stakes environments.

Second, training data bias poses risks of perpetuating insecure coding practices. Models trained on open-source repositories may learn and reproduce security vulnerabilities present in the training data. Third, the computational cost of running large AI models remains prohibitive for smaller development teams and resource-constrained environments.

Fourth, intellectual property concerns surrounding AI-generated code continue to evolve, with legal frameworks lagging behind technological capabilities. The question of copyright ownership for code generated by AI models trained on copyrighted source code remains unresolved in most jurisdictions [15].

VI. Conclusion

This survey has examined the current state of AI integration in software engineering, revealing both significant achievements and persistent challenges. AI-driven tools have demonstrated measurable improvements in developer productivity, code quality, and testing effectiveness across multiple studies. The most impactful applications are in code generation and testing automation, where mature tools are already widely deployed in industry.

Future research should focus on improving the reliability of AI-generated code for complex systems, developing better evaluation frameworks for AI-assisted development tools, and addressing the ethical and legal implications of widespread AI adoption in software engineering. As AI capabilities continue to advance, the software engineering discipline must evolve its practices, education, and standards to effectively harness these technologies while mitigating associated risks.
